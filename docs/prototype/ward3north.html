The building

Northern Michigan Asylum opened on November 30, 1885, the state's third asylum, built to take pressure off the crowded hospitals at Kalamazoo and Pontiac.

Its first and largest building, Building 50, was designed by Gordon W. Lloyd on the Kirkbride plan in a Victorian Italianate style, from yellow-white brick fired at the Markham brickworks in nearby Greilickville.

The building forms a shallow V. Its wings step back from the center so that every ward had a view of Grand Traverse Bay. Women lived in the north wings and men in the south.

Kirkbride's model ward had its own parlor, a dining room served by a dumbwaiter from the central kitchen, single bedrooms off a wide corridor, a small dormitory watched from an attendant's room, and a bath room.

In 1963 the original center section was torn down as a fire hazard. The hospital closed in 1989 after serving more than 50,000 patients, and the buildings sat vacant for about a decade.

Below the grounds runs a network of brick tunnels. Large fans forced air through them into the basements and up flues in the walls, part of the hospital's ventilation system.

Restoration began in 2002. The north end was the last part redeveloped. A reporter who walked it in 2013 found peeling paint, floors and ceilings missing in places, and walls more than two feet thick.

About this ward

Ward 3 North is built for this demo. Its layout follows Kirkbride's published recommendations for a ward at roughly real scale (a 36 m corridor, 4 m ceilings), not a measured drawing of Building 50. The stairs and service tunnel under the old bath room are sketched from explorers' photos of the real tunnels, not from plans. Margaret Hale and the people in her notes are invented. A full game would build each wing from the original floor plans.
